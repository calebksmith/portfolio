## Problem

Web, mobile, and desktop each had their own login, and getting in was our top
support issue.

Sign-up was worse. Each organization has its own way in, like single sign-on, an
email allowlist, or a registration code. Users didn't know which was theirs.

> **The call:** a code, not a magic link. We designed the link first, but it
> assumes the device reading the email is the device signing in — and for a
> utility crew sharing a terminal, it usually isn't. A six-digit code travels
> between devices. A link doesn't.

## What I did

- **Started with research.** Interviews, support tickets, and the five whys
  showed people weren't locked out by security. They forgot a password, or which
  email they'd signed up with. I explored options and tested the design with
  customers.
- **Designed for forgetting.** "Remember me" keeps your email, and a forgotten
  password gets a code by email, not a reset. Changing a password you've
  forgotten is a chore invented by the login screen, not a security
  requirement.
- **One flow, one service.** One web login, shown in the mobile and desktop apps
  through a webview. Three codebases became one.
- **Asked "who do you work for?"** instead of "which registration type is
  yours?" Pick your company and get its flow. The list stays empty until you
  type three letters and includes decoys, so nobody can use it to list our
  customers.
- **Designed the admin side.** Admins see who hasn't signed up and invite them.
  About two in three active organizations used it in its first eight months.

## Outcome

- **Login and account-access support tickets dropped 80%.**
- Users hitting a broken password-reset link fell more than 90%, and sign-ins
  held steady through the switch.
- Security went up — email verification, MFA — without adding steps to the
  common path.

> **Trade-offs:** a webview isn't native; we took slightly less polish for one
> flow instead of three, the right call for a small team. Biometric sign-in was
> prototyped and cut to ship sooner. It's the first thing I'd put back.

**Who did what:** I led the UX research, customer interviews, and UI.
Engineering built the flows and the service; product management and leadership
shaped the verification requirements.
